Abstract—To address cross-asset attack propagation and lim-
ited defense capacity in multi-asset networks, this paper estab-
lishes a complete-information, nonzero-sum, open-loop differen-
tial game. Asset-level compromised fractions describe network-
state evolution, while isolation and recovery controls specify
the defender’s resource allocation over time. Continuous-time
strategies are parameterized using piecewise-constant controls.
By combining fourth-order Runge–Kutta integration, sequential
least-squares programming, and alternating best responses, Alter-
nating Best Response for Dynamic Defense Action Decision (ABR-
DAD) is developed to calculate isolation and recovery intensities
for each asset and time interval. A directed multi-asset prop-
agation network is constructed to examine the spatiotemporal
allocation of resources under different defense capacities. Dy-
namic defense, fixed-mean defense, and no defense are compared
in terms of compromised states and cumulative losses under
the same time-varying attack sequence. Simulations show that
the computed plans satisfy instantaneous capacity constraints
and yield differentiated resource allocation. In the main-capacity
comparison under the shared attack plan generated with dy-
namic defense, the dynamic plan reduces cumulative weighted
compromise loss and total cumulative defender loss relative to
both reference schemes. These results support the method for
computing defense schedules under finite resources.
Keywords—network defense decision-making, nonzero-sum dif-
ferential game, alternating best response, attack propagation,
resource allocation
I. INTRODUCTION
A multi-asset network comprises interrelated logical assets,
including entry services, business processing, identity authen-
tication, and core data. Attacks can propagate from entry
points to downstream assets along directed paths, causing
compromise across multiple assets and business losses. A
defender can suppress outgoing propagation through source-
side isolation or reduce local compromise through recovery.
These actions differ in security effects, business impacts, and
resource consumption. Under limited capacity, the defender
must account for the attacker’s strategy when determining the
type, intensity, and timing of defensive actions.
Regarding network propagation control and resource allo-
cation, Preciado et al.[1] studied the allocation of protection
resources to inhibit spreading processes in directed networks.
Al-Eiadeh and Abdallah[2] allocated defense resources to
interdependent systems using attack graphs and PageRank.
Paarporn and Xu[3] examined resource tradeoffs between pre-
ventive and reactive defenses, while Chen et al.[4] investigated
the effects of protection and recovery investments and node
priorities on network robustness. These studies provide a basis
for characterizing propagation structure, asset importance, and
defense costs.
In network attack–defense games, Shao and Li[5] used a mul-
tistage attack–defense graph to examine resource allocation
during sequential attacks. Hu et al.[6] optimized moving-target
defense through a Markov differential game, and Lou et al.[7]
studied dynamic collaborative defense in crowdsensing net-
works under incomplete information. Huang and Zhu[8] used a
susceptible–infected–susceptible (SIS) propagation model and
a differential-game framework to study virus suppression and
adaptive adjustment of connection weights in complex net-
works. Mi et al.[9] introduced stochastic disturbances into net-
work attack–defense state evolution and developed a stochastic
differential-game model for defense strategy selection. Tian
et al.[10] studied a nonzero-sum attack–defense game for
malware propagation in cloud environments. Zhang et al.
investigated real-time defense in scale-free networks[11] and
potential differential-game decisions in complex networks[12].
Sun et al.[13] incorporated action and timing choices into a time
differential game. These works provide methodological foun-
dations for analyzing strategic interactions alongside network-
state evolution.
For post-attack repair, Yang et al.[14] employed a differential
game to study host repair under advanced persistent threats
(APTs). Wang et al.[15] examined APT lateral movement,
repair delays, and resource constraints in time-varying net-
works. Qin et al.[16] studied defense outsourcing under lim-
ited budgets through an impulsive differential game. Existing
work thus covers propagation control, strategic interaction,
and dynamic repair. This study further addresses the joint
spatiotemporal allocation of source-side isolation and local
recovery in a directed propagation network under attacker
strategy responses and finite capacity constraints.
This paper undertakes three tasks: constructing a nonzero-
sum, open-loop differential-game model involving directed
propagation, source-side isolation, and local recovery; design-
ing ABR-DAD using piecewise-constant controls and alternat-
ing best responses to compute defensive actions by asset and
interval; and analyzing allocations across defense capacities
while comparing dynamic, fixed-mean, and no-defense plans
under a common attack sequence in terms of compromised
states and cumulative losses.

plan: holding the attack plan fixed, optimize isolation and
recovery; then, holding the defense plan fixed, compute the
attack response; and alternate until the numerical stopping
conditions are met. The procedure is shown in Algorithm 1.
The maximum number of alternating iterations is 𝑀= 40;
each SLSQP solve allows at most 300 iterations with a
tolerance of 10−10. Complete alternating runs start from uni-
form feasible, zero-control, and random feasible plans, while

its sole starting point. The stopping metrics are normalized
discovered unilateral improvement 𝜂𝑝, projected-gradient sta-

B. Defense Resource Allocation
The experiment first reports the defender’s resource in-
puts and concrete actions across control intervals to assess
whether the method produces capacity-feasible defense sched-
ules across assets and time intervals. Under 𝐵𝐷= 1.2, G uses
capacities 0.843705, 1.200000, 1.200000, 1.200000, 1.065570,
and 0.337751 in the six intervals. The capacity bound is active
in intervals 2–4 and slack in the others. With 𝐵𝐷= 0.6,
capacity is fully used in the first five intervals; with 𝐵𝐷= 1.8,
all intervals retain slack, as shown in Figure 2. Input decreases
in the last interval under all three capacities, reflecting the
tradeoff between finite-horizon protection benefits and action
costs. Accordingly, the defender allocates capacity in view of
state evolution and action costs rather than applying fixed-
proportion static allocation; the plan exhibits both binding
capacity constraints and reduced late-horizon control effort.
Table 2 presents mean allocations by asset under different
capacities. As defense capacity increases, the absolute input
to the API gateway grows but its share of total network
resources declines; both the input and share of identity au-
thentication increase. Thus, defense resources expand from
a relative concentration on entry assets to other assets with
propagation influence, indicating dynamic adjustment across
assets under finite resources. Figure 2 captures interval-level
capacity use, whereas Table 2 reports asset-level allocation.
Figure 3 shows isolation and recovery intensities for six assets
over six intervals at 𝐵𝐷= 1.2, totaling 72 defense-control
components, and explicitly identifies action locations, types,
and intensities.
The API gateway serves as an attack entry point and
connects to order processing and identity authentication, so
isolation and recovery are applied together in early intervals.
Identity authentication has considerable outgoing propagation
influence; its isolation intensity rises in intervals 3–5 and
declines in the final interval. The core database has a high
loss weight but no outgoing edges and primarily receives local
recovery. These allocations accord with the assets’ propagation
positions and the defensive mechanisms.
