Abstract—The increasing complexity of modern telecommunications
networks introduces security challenges that require systematic threat
analysis. However, a gap remains between architectural threat modeling
frameworks, such as Spoofing, Tampering, Repudiation, Information
Disclosure, Denial of Service, and Elevation of Privilege (STRIDE), and
operational knowledge bases, such as the MITRE Five-G Hierarchy
of Threats (FiGHT). This gap limits the ability to connect architectural
weaknesses with specific protocol behaviors and attack sequences.
This paper presents Multi-stage Attack Surface Analysis and Rea-
soning (MASAR), a formal framework that bridges this gap through
three layers: strategic threat mapping, logical attack path decomposition
using directed acyclic graphs (DAGs), and operational synthesis of
tactics, techniques, and procedures (TTPs). MASAR is evaluated using
a 5G-ready IP Multimedia Subsystem (IMS) architecture, examining two
signaling attack scenarios: early media billing bypass through Session
Initiation Protocol (SIP) signaling and subscriber information disclosure
and topology discovery through the Diameter Cx interface. MiniZinc
constraint models are used to analyze the reachability of unsafe protocol
states. The analysis demonstrates how MASAR connects architectural
threats with specific protocol behaviors and supports security controls
that account for network states in telecommunications systems.
Index Terms—Threat Modeling Methodology, Telecom Cybersecurity,
Cross-Layer Synthesis, MITRE FiGHT, STRIDE, TTP Formalization.

INTRODUCTION
The evolution of telecommunications networks toward cloud-
native architectures has introduced new security challenges. Fifth
Generation (5G) networks rely on a Service-Based Architecture
(SBA), where network functions communicate through standard-
ized interfaces and Application Programming Interfaces (APIs),
as defined by the Third Generation Partnership Project (3GPP)
in TS 23.501 [1]. Although these technologies improve network
flexibility and service deployment, they also introduce additional
interfaces, dependencies, and potential attack paths. Recent studies
have highlighted security threats affecting different 5G network
domains and the challenges associated with their increasingly
complex architectures [2], [3].
The security architecture defined in 3GPP TS 33.501 provides
mechanisms for protecting network functions and their commu-
nication interfaces [4]. However, assessing individual security
mechanisms does not necessarily reveal how weaknesses across
multiple interfaces can be combined into a complete attack
sequence. Recent research has emphasized the importance of
analyzing interconnected vulnerabilities and multistage attacks in
5G environments [5]. This creates a need for threat analysis meth-
ods that consider both architectural weaknesses and the protocol
behaviors through which attacks may occur.
Existing security approaches address these challenges from
different perspectives. Architectural threat modeling methods,
such as Spoofing, Tampering, Repudiation, Information Disclo-
sure, Denial of Service, and Elevation of Privilege (STRIDE)
[6], and the Process for Attack Simulation and Threat Analysis
(PASTA) [7], help identify potential threats and weaknesses in
system design. However, translating these findings into specific
protocol actions and state transitions often requires additional tech-
nical analysis. In contrast, operational knowledge bases, including
the MITRE Five-G Hierarchy of Threats (FiGHT) [8], organize
known adversarial behaviors into tactics and techniques. Although
both approaches are valuable, they serve different purposes and
do not independently provide a complete process for tracing
architectural threats to executable protocol sequences.
This distinction motivates the development of Multi-stage
Attack Surface Analysis and Reasoning (MASAR), a formal threat
modeling framework that connects architectural threat identifi-
cation with detailed protocol analysis. MASAR follows three
layers: strategic threat mapping, logical attack path decomposition,
and operational synthesis of tactics, techniques, and procedures
(TTPs). Directed acyclic graphs (DAGs) are used to represent
attack dependencies, while constraint-based models examine the
reachability of unsafe protocol states.
Traditional threat modeling approaches provide useful meth-
ods for identifying security concerns, but their application to com-
plex telecommunications systems often requires substantial analyst
interpretation. A single communication session may involve sev-
eral network functions, signaling protocols, and state transitions.

Consequently, an architectural threat may remain insufficiently
understood unless the conditions and sequence of actions required
for exploitation are also examined.
Formal modeling provides a structured way to represent these
relationships. Graph-based approaches support the analysis of
attack dependencies and potential paths through interconnected
system components [9]. In MASAR, formal threat analysis pro-
vides three main benefits:
•
Structured Threat Decomposition: Architectural threats
are represented through logical attack paths, allowing their
underlying conditions and technical actions to be examined
systematically.
•
Analysis of Protocol States and Dependencies: Formal
models represent relationships between protocol actions
and system states, supporting the identification of potential
attack sequences that may not be apparent from isolated
security checks.
•
Support for Security Verification: Constraint-based
analysis enables the examination of security properties and
the identification of reachable unsafe states under explicitly
defined assumptions.
The MASAR framework applies these principles to a 5G-ready
Internet Protocol (IP) Multimedia Subsystem (IMS) architecture.
The evaluation examines two signaling attack scenarios: early-
media billing bypass involving the Session Initiation Protocol
(SIP), and unauthorized subscriber and routing information disclo-
sure through the Diameter Cx interface. These scenarios illustrate
how architectural trust assumptions and protocol interactions can
be analyzed to identify potential security weaknesses.
The primary contributions of this paper are summarized as
follows:
•
MASAR Threat Modeling Framework: A three-layer
methodology connecting architectural threat identification,
logical attack path decomposition, and operational threat
analysis.
•
Formal Attack Path Analysis: A graph-based represen-
tation of attack dependencies, combined with constraint
models for examining protocol states and potential viola-
tions of security properties.
•
IMS Security Evaluation: An application of MASAR to
two signaling attack scenarios, illustrating the relationship
between architectural weaknesses, protocol behaviors, and
potential security controls.
The remainder of this paper is organized as follows. Section 2
reviews existing research on architectural threat modeling, telecom
security knowledge bases, attack graph analysis, and protocol
security. Section 3 presents the MASAR methodology, including
strategic threat mapping, logical attack path decomposition, and
operational TTP synthesis.
Section 4 evaluates MASAR using two IMS signaling attack
scenarios: early-media billing bypass through SIP and unautho-
rized information disclosure through Diameter Cx.
Section 5 presents the constraint-based models, security prop-
erties, and verification results demonstrating unsafe-state reacha-
bility under the specified model assumptions.
Section 6 discusses the findings, limitations, and proposed
architectural countermeasures. Finally, Section 7 concludes the
paper and outlines directions for future research.

RELATED WORK
Research relevant to MASAR spans architectural threat modeling,
telecommunications security knowledge bases, graph-based attack
analysis, and empirical protocol security research. These areas
provide complementary perspectives, ranging from identifying
architectural weaknesses and classifying adversarial behavior to
analyzing attack dependencies and protocol-level vulnerabilities.
Table 1 categorizes the relevant methodologies, knowledge
bases, security guidance, and modeling techniques according to
their primary purposes and relationships to MASAR.
Building on these complementary approaches, MASAR inte-
grates architectural threat identification, logical attack path decom-
position, and operational TTP synthesis within a structured three-
layer methodology. Constraint- based modeling further supports
the examination of protocol states and unsafe-state reachability
under explicitly defined assumptions.
2.1
Architectural Threat Modeling Frameworks
Architectural threat modeling supports the systematic identifica-
tion of security weaknesses in system components, interfaces, and
trust boundaries. Farooqui et al. [15] present a layered threat model
for 5G systems that maps threats to architectural layers, affected
technologies, and potential entry points. STRIDE [6] provides
a structured classification of security threats, while PASTA [7]
incorporates business objectives, technical risks, and attack sce-
narios. Although these methods provide valuable foundations for
security assessment, translating architectural findings into specific
signaling procedures and protocol state transitions requires further
analysis.
Recent research has examined security threats across increas-
ingly complex telecommunications architectures. Tariq et al. [3]
investigate security threats in private 5G and beyond 5G networks
through layered analysis. Breeden et al. [5] examine security
vulnerabilities in 5G network slicing and the relationships between
potential attack scenarios. These studies highlight the importance
of considering dependencies across network components rather
than treating security threats as isolated events.
Graph-based approaches provide structured representations of
attack dependencies. Kordy et al. [16] examine foundations for
attack and defense modeling, while Wide l et al. [17] survey
formal methods for analyzing and generating attack trees. Konsta
et al. [18] review approaches for automatically generating attack
trees and attack graphs. More recently, Palma et al. [12] propose
a scalable approach to attack graph generation that supports
progressive analysis of potential attack paths. Zhang et al. [19]
investigate automated attack graph generation using graph neural
networks and Bayesian models for industrial networks. Similarly,
Saint-Hilaire et al. [13] present an approach for generating attack
and defense graphs to support the analysis of security events and
potential countermeasures.
2.2
Telecom Knowledge Bases and Matrices
Telecommunications security knowledge bases provide structured
representations of adversarial behavior and support threat identifi-
cation, classification, and security operations. MITRE FiGHT [8]
organizes tactics and techniques relevant to 5G networks. Simi-
larly, the Bhadra framework [10] provides a taxonomy of threats
affecting mobile communication systems. The GSM Association
(GSMA) FS.31 document [11] complements these resources by
providing baseline security controls for mobile network operators.

Recent research has extended telecommunications security
analysis beyond threat classification. Gong et al. [2] systematically
examine attacks affecting standalone 5G networks, including user
equipment, radio access networks, and core network components.
Jelba and Sajan [20] propose a security framework for detecting
threats across the control and user planes of cloud-native 5G
networks. Their approach illustrates how relationships between
signaling events and network behavior can support security moni-
toring.
These contributions provide valuable foundations for under-
standing telecommunications threats and identifying adversarial
behavior. However, threat classification, operational detection,
and architectural threat decomposition address different stages of
security analysis. MASAR focuses on connecting these stages by
mapping architectural weaknesses to logical attack paths and de-
riving protocol actions that can support operational threat analysis.
2.3
Empirical Protocol Security Research
Empirical telecommunications security research has identified
vulnerabilities affecting mobile communication protocols and ser-
vices. Rupprecht et al. [21] demonstrate the ReVoLTE attack,
which exploits keystream reuse in Voice over Long-Term Evolu-
tion (VoLTE) communications. Lee et al. [22] introduce VWAna-
lyzer to systematically examine security weaknesses in Voice over
Wi-Fi (VoWiFi) protocols. While initial IMS research focused on
VoLTE, modern 5G Standalone (SA) deployments rely on Voice
over New Radio (VoNR), where the IMS core remains the central
control plane for media session establishment over 5G Core (5GC)
SBI and N5/Rx interfaces.
Research involving IMS has also demonstrated weaknesses
associated with signaling procedures and session management.
Li et al. [23] investigate vulnerabilities in operational VoLTE
networks, including signaling and billing related attacks. Lu et
al. [24] examine ghost calls and demonstrate how IMS signaling
behavior can be exploited to disrupt communication services.
These findings highlight the importance of analyzing protocol in-
teractions and session states when evaluating telecommunications
security.
Recent research provides additional perspectives on complex
attack behavior. Gong et al. [2] review attacks across standalone
5G architectures, while Saint-Hilaire et al. [13] present an auto-
mated approach for generating attack and defense graphs. Their
framework connects attack relationships with potential counter-
measures, demonstrating the value of linking security analysis with
operational response.
Although existing studies provide substantial evidence of pro-
tocol vulnerabilities and advanced attack modeling techniques,
their objectives differ from the architectural transformation process
proposed in MASAR. The framework combines strategic threat
mapping, logical path decomposition, and operational TTP synthe-
sis. Constraint-based analysis is then applied to examine whether
selected protocol scenarios can reach unsafe states under specified
assumptions.
Together, the reviewed studies establish foundations for archi-
tectural threat analysis, graph-based security modeling, telecom
threat intelligence, and empirical protocol evaluation. MASAR
integrates these perspectives into a methodology focused on the
relationships between architectural threats, protocol behavior, and
security properties.

METHODOLOGY: CROSS-LAYER SYNTHESIS
The MASAR framework provides a structured methodology for
connecting architectural security threats with specific protocol be-
haviors and operational threat information. As illustrated in Fig. 1,
the methodology consists of three layers: (1) Strategic Threat
Mapping, (2) Logical Path Decomposition, and (3) Operational
TTP Synthesis. Each layer builds on the output of the previous
layer to establish a traceable relationship between architectural
weaknesses, potential attack paths, and adversarial techniques.
3.1
Layer 1: Strategic Threat Mapping
The first layer identifies potential security threats by examining the
functional architecture, network components, interfaces, and trust
boundaries. STRIDE [6] is used to classify threats associated with
each component and its interactions with other network functions.
Let N = {n1, n2, . . . , nk} represent the set of network
components, and let S denote the set of STRIDE threat categories.
The strategic mapping function is defined as (Eq.1):
τ : N →P(S),
(1)
where P(S) represents the power set of the STRIDE cate-
gories. For each component ni ∈N, the function τ(ni) identifies
the set of potential threats associated with that component.
This mapping provides a structured basis for examining archi-
tectural weaknesses, such as insufficient validation of signaling
messages, implicit trust between network functions, or inadequate
protection of communication interfaces. Identified threats are

3.2
Layer 2: Logical Path Decomposition
The second layer translates architectural threats into structured
attack paths by examining the conditions and protocol actions
required to reach an attacker objective. DAGs are used to represent
dependencies between attack steps, while AND and OR gates
describe their logical relationships. This representation builds on
established approaches to attack and defense modeling [16], [18].
MASAR represents attack dependencies using directed acyclic
graphs (DAGs), with attack trees treated as a restricted hierarchical
form of this representation. While attack trees organize prereq-
uisites into distinct branches, the general DAG representation
also supports shared dependencies across multiple attack paths.
Consequently, the logical evaluation rules adopted by MASAR
apply to both representations.
Recent research has further demonstrated the value of formal
and logical attack graph analysis. Catta et al. [25] present a formal
approach for examining attack paths that may lead to prohibited
system states. Their work uses game theoretic modeling and
automata based verification to reason about system vulnerability.
Similarly, Tayouri et al. [26] introduce CORAL, a framework that
uses logical attack graphs to assess security risks in dynamic
container environments. These studies illustrate how graph repre-
sentations can support structured security analysis across complex
system components.
For each identified threat s ∈τ(ni), the relevant architectural
conditions and protocol interactions are examined to construct
an attack graph. The attacker objective is represented by the
root node, while prerequisite conditions and protocol actions are
represented by the remaining nodes. Dependencies are established
according to the conditions required to satisfy each attack objec-
tive.
The resulting attack graph is represented as (Eq. 2):
Gs = (V, E),
(2)
where V is the finite set of nodes representing attack actions
or logical conditions, and E ⊆V × V is the set of directed
dependency edges.
Each directed edge (u, v) ∈E indicates that node u con-
tributes to satisfying the condition represented by node v. The
prerequisite nodes of v are therefore defined as (Eq. 3):
ch(v) = {u ∈V | (u, v) ∈E}.
(3)
Each node is assigned a Boolean state x(v) ∈{0, 1}, where
x(v) = 1 indicates that its corresponding attack condition is sat-
isfied. Leaf nodes represent atomic protocol actions or conditions,
while internal nodes combine their dependencies through AND or
OR logic.

Its objective is to connect technical behavior represented in the
attack graph with relevant adversarial tactics and techniques.
Each identified action is examined in relation to existing
telecom threat knowledge bases, particularly MITRE FiGHT [8].
This analysis considers the associated protocol, attacker objective,
required conditions, and relationship to existing techniques.

The transformation pipeline shown in Fig. 2 connects archi-
tectural threat identification with logical attack decomposition and
constraint-based verification. First, a STRIDE threat s ∈τ(ni)
is associated with an attacker objective represented by the root
node of an attack DAG Gs. The objective is decomposed into
prerequisite conditions and protocol actions through analysis of
the relevant network architecture, trust boundaries, and applicable
protocol specifications. Mandatory prerequisites are represented
through AND dependencies, whereas alternative conditions are
represented through OR dependencies.
Second, the attack graph provides a structured basis for identi-
fying the protocol states, conditions, and events relevant to formal
verification. These elements are represented using finite-domain
variables and constraints in MiniZinc. Logical dependencies are
modeled through Boolean constraints, while temporal ordering
and state transitions are specified separately according to the
relevant protocol behavior. The resulting constraint satisfaction
problem is evaluated against a defined safety property Psafe.
To identify a potential unsafe execution, the solver searches for
a satisfying assignment to ¬Psafe under the specified model
assumptions and execution bounds.
In the current implementation, the transformation from
STRIDE threat categories to attack DAGs and the corresponding
MiniZinc constraint models is performed manually by security
researchers. The process follows a structured, analyst-guided
methodology based on architectural analysis, protocol specifica-
tions, and identified attack dependencies. Automated generation of
attack DAGs and executable constraint models is outside the scope
of the present implementation and is identified as a direction for
future work.
When an identified protocol action corresponds to an existing
technique, it is associated with the relevant knowledge base entry.
Otherwise, the action may be documented as a candidate tech-
nique, together with its protocol context, required conditions, and
potential security implications.
This distinction separates the identification of potentially
unclassified behavior from the confirmation of a novel attack
technique. Establishing novelty requires further comparison with
existing research, documented vulnerabilities, and relevant threat
knowledge bases.
The synthesis process establishes a traceable relationship be-
tween architectural threats and operational security information.
Its outputs can support the development of detection requirements,
security analysis procedures, and architectural countermeasures.

3.4
Algorithmic Representation of the Transformation
Process
The MASAR transformation process combines logical attack
graph analysis with operational technique mapping. Algorithm 1
provides a procedural representation of the methodology, includ-
ing the extraction of atomic actions, evaluation of attack depen-
dencies, and identification of candidate operational techniques.
The algorithm describes the analytical steps followed by se-
curity researchers rather than an implemented automated trans-
formation system. In the current evaluation, STRIDE threat map-
ping, attack DAG construction, and MiniZinc constraint model
development are performed manually. Automating these stages is
identified as a direction for future work.
For each attack graph, the algorithm first identifies the leaf
nodes representing atomic protocol actions or conditions. It then
evaluates the remaining nodes according to their logical depen-
dencies using a topological ordering of the DAG. This ensures
that prerequisite conditions are evaluated before the nodes that
depend on them, following established graph traversal principles
[27].
The resulting atomic actions are examined against existing
technique definitions to determine whether they correspond to
known adversarial behavior or require further assessment.
The algorithm provides a structured representation of the
relationship between logical attack dependencies and operational
threat information. It does not independently establish protocol
exploitability or the novelty of identified techniques. These proper-
ties require further validation through protocol constraints, formal
verification, and comparison with existing security research.

4.2
Attack Vector 1: Early Media Free Ride (Zero An-
swer Fraud)
This scenario examines how interactions between SIP session es-
tablishment, early-media authorization, and charging enforcement
can create a potential opportunity for unauthorized media con-
sumption. The analysis first establishes the technical assumptions
and attacker capabilities, then decomposes the attack into logical
conditions, and finally interprets the resulting behavior from an
operational threat perspective.
4.2.1

The first stage establishes the protocol context and configuration

coordination of SIP session-state management, media authoriza-
tion, and charging enforcement. Its feasibility must be established
against the specific IMS configuration and charging mechanisms
under evaluation; it should not be assumed to arise from the use
of early media alone.

The final stage interprets the identified protocol actions in relation

This step connects the logical attack conditions with candidate
technique descriptions without assuming that the behavior consti-

technique.
The early-media scenario involves manipulation of provisional
SIP session behavior to maintain media transmission without the
expected charging event. The relevant actions include maintaining

These behaviors are examined in relation to telecommuni-

The analysis also identifies potential security control require-

4.3
Attack Vector 2: Unauthorized Diameter Cx Infor-
mation Disclosure
The second scenario examines potential disclosure of subscriber
authorization or internal IMS routing information through the
Diameter Cx interface. As in the first scenario, the analysis begins
with the technical preconditions, then identifies the logical attack
path, and finally connects the observed protocol behavior with
operational threat information.
4.3.1
Technical Scenario and Attack Preconditions
This stage identifies the network functions, signaling procedures,
and authorization assumptions relevant to the modeled information
disclosure scenario. The primary concern is whether a Diameter
peer lacking the required authorization can submit subscriber-
related requests and receive information intended for trusted IMS
signaling entities.
The Cx interface supports communication between IMS con-
trol functions and subscriber information systems. The User-
Authorization-Request (UAR) and User-Authorization-Answer
(UAA) procedures are defined in 3GPP TS 29.229 [31].
The modeled security concern arises when an unauthorized
Diameter peer can issue subscriber authorization requests that
are accepted by the network and obtain response information that
reveals subscriber status or internal routing details.
Under normal operation, the Interrogating Call Session Con-
trol Function (I-CSCF) exchanges Diameter messages with the
Home Subscriber Server (HSS) through the Cx interface. The
underlying Diameter protocol specifies peer relationships and
message exchange procedures [32].
A UAR carries information required for the subscriber autho-
rization procedure, and the corresponding UAA returns the result
and associated information defined by the applicable protocol
procedure.
The scenario assumes that an adversary has obtained access
to a signaling path capable of reaching the relevant Diameter
service and can cause requests to be accepted as originating
from an authorized peer. This access and acceptance condition
is an explicit prerequisite of the modeled attack, not an inherent
property of the Cx interface.
The adversary generates a UAR containing a target subscriber
identity. If the Diameter edge infrastructure accepts and forwards
the request, the HSS processes it according to the applicable
authorization procedure.
The response may provide information that helps distinguish
subscriber authorization outcomes. Depending on the procedure
and response content, additional routing information may also be
exposed.
Repeated queries could therefore support subscriber presence
enumeration or reveal information about the internal IMS topology
when peer authorization and information disclosure controls are
insufficient.
This scenario highlights the distinction between Diameter
peer authorization and SIP-level subscriber authentication. These
mechanisms operate at different stages of IMS signaling and
provide different security functions; the presence of one does not
automatically establish the effectiveness of the other.
Two information-disclosure opportunities are considered:
•
Subscriber Authorization Enumeration: Differences be-
tween successful authorization outcomes and subscriber-
related error responses may reveal whether a queried
identity is recognized or satisfies particular authorization
conditions.

4.3.3
Operational Interpretation and TTP Mapping
The final stage examines how the modeled Diameter signaling
behavior relates to operational threat information. Consistent with
MASAR Layer 3, the objective is to interpret the protocol actions
and identify potential technique descriptions rather than assume
that every modeled behavior represents a novel or confirmed
attack.
The use of unauthorized Diameter queries to infer subscriber
information or routing characteristics may be documented as a
candidate reconnaissance technique.
More specifically, the behavior is characterized in this study as
the candidate technique Unauthorized Diameter Cx Subscriber and
Topology Information Disclosure, subject to further assessment
against existing telecom threat knowledge bases, including MITRE
FiGHT [8].
The scenario also identifies potential control requirements,
including Diameter peer authentication, signaling access restric-
tions, request authorization, and validation of information returned
across trust boundaries.
The corresponding protocol ordering assumptions and security
properties are examined through the constraint model presented in
Section 5.
5
FORMAL VERIFICATION VIA CONSTRAINT SAT-
ISFACTION
To examine the feasibility of the attack scenarios identified by
MASAR, constraint satisfaction is used to analyze protocol states
and their relationships. The verification process represents relevant
signaling procedures as finite sets of variables and constraints, al-
lowing the reachability of potentially unsafe states to be examined.
The analysis focuses on two scenarios introduced in Section 4:
Early Media Free Ride and unauthorized Diameter Cx information
disclosure. The first examines the relationship between SIP session
states, media authorization, and charging. The second examines
whether insufficient Diameter peer authorization enforcement can
permit protected subscriber or routing information to be disclosed
to an unauthorized requester.
Each scenario is represented as a constraint satisfaction prob-
lem (CSP). A security property is defined to describe the expected
safe behavior, and its negation is used to search for a violating
execution trace. A satisfiable result indicates that a violating state
is reachable under the modeled constraints. An unsatisfiable result
indicates that no such state exists within the specified model and
search bounds.
The models are implemented using MiniZinc [14], a constraint
modeling language that supports finite-domain variables and log-
ical constraints. The corresponding executable specifications are
provided in Appendix A.
For consistency, both models are presented using four analyt-
ical stages: model representation and assumptions, protocol con-
straints, security properties and reachability analysis, and witness
trace and result interpretation. Although the models address dif-
ferent signaling protocols, this structure provides a common basis
for describing their formulation and interpreting their outcomes.
5.1
Model 1: Early Media Billing Bypass
The first model examines a potential inconsistency between SIP
session progression, media authorization, and charging enforce-
ment. It considers a network configuration in which media trans-
mission is permitted during a provisional session state, while the
charging event under investigation is associated with final session
confirmation.
The objective is to determine whether the modeled session can
remain in a provisional state with media transmission authorized
beyond a specified duration without the expected charging event.
This analysis evaluates the reachability of the defined unsafe
condition under the model’s assumptions; it does not, by itself,
establish that the same behavior occurs in every IMS implementa-
tion.

The integration of these stages enables MASAR to connect
STRIDE-based architectural threat identification with logical at-
tack decomposition, protocol-state analysis, and operational TTP
synthesis. Accordingly, MASAR should be understood as an ex-
tension of architectural threat modeling rather than an alternative
to STRIDE.
The table describes methodological capabilities rather than
experimentally established performance improvements. Quanti-
tative conclusions regarding effectiveness, scalability, or analyst
consistency require further evaluation.
6
DISCUSSION: GAP ANALYSIS AND ARCHITEC-
TURAL COUNTERMEASURES
The MASAR methodology aims to connect architectural security
threats with specific protocol behaviors and operational security
requirements. The IMS evaluation illustrates how interactions
between signaling procedures, network states, and trust boundaries
can create security risks that are not fully described by architec-
tural threat classification alone.
The two examined scenarios highlight different security con-
cerns. Early Media Free Ride involves the relationship between
session establishment, media authorization, and charging, while
Unauthorized Diameter Cx Information Disclosure concerns the
authorization of Diameter signaling requests and the potential
exposure of subscriber or internal IMS routing information. Both
scenarios demonstrate the importance of examining protocol con-
ditions alongside architectural security controls.
6.1
The Signaling Plane Security Gap
Telecommunications security frameworks provide valuable meth-
ods for identifying and classifying threats across network compo-
nents. However, classifying a threat does not necessarily explain
the signaling sequence or system conditions required for its occur-
rence.

13
The IMS scenarios illustrate this distinction. In the Early
Media Free Ride scenario, the potential weakness arises from
the relationship between provisional SIP signaling, media au-
thorization, and charging policies. The security risk depends on
whether media transmission can continue without the expected
charging event or an appropriate duration restriction. Such risks
are also recognized in the SIP early media security considerations
described in RFC 3960 [28].
Similarly, the Unauthorized Diameter Cx Information Disclo-
sure scenario concerns the relationship between Diameter peer au-
thorization and subscriber information access. Potential disclosure
occurs when an unauthorized requesting entity receives Diameter
authorization responses without sufficient enforcement of the rele-
vant trust boundaries. Depending on the returned information, this
may enable subscriber enumeration or reveal internal IMS routing
characteristics.
These scenarios demonstrate why threat classification and pro-
tocol analysis should be considered together. MASAR addresses
this requirement by connecting architectural threat categories
with logical attack dependencies and the corresponding protocol
conditions.
6.2
The Role of Constraint Solving in Protocol Security
Analysis
Constraint solving provides a structured approach for examin-
ing relationships between protocol states, signaling events, and
security properties. It allows potential attack conditions to be
represented as logical constraints and evaluated within a defined
model.
In MASAR, constraint satisfaction is used to examine whether
selected signaling scenarios can reach states that violate specified
security requirements. The resulting assignments provide infor-
mation about the conditions and event sequences associated with
those states.
This approach offers three main benefits:
•
Structured Representation of Protocol Behavior: Proto-
col states, events, and security conditions can be expressed
as variables and logical constraints. This provides a con-
sistent representation of the interactions examined in each
attack scenario.
•
Connection to Logical Attack Decomposition: The
atomic actions and dependencies identified in the MASAR
attack graphs provide a basis for defining relevant protocol
conditions. Constraint models can then examine the reach-
ability of selected unsafe states under these conditions.
•
Support for Reproducible Security Analysis: Explicit
variables, constraints, and solver configurations allow the
same model to be evaluated independently. This supports
the examination of security properties under clearly de-
fined assumptions.
However, the conclusions obtained through constraint solving
depend on the completeness and accuracy of the model. A satis-
fying assignment establishes the reachability of a state under the
specified constraints, but it does not independently demonstrate
that the same behavior occurs in every implementation.
Similarly, bounded verification does not establish the absence
of vulnerabilities outside the modeled execution limits. The results
should therefore be interpreted in relation to the selected protocol
assumptions, state variables, and verification bounds.
Future extensions could incorporate additional protocol con-
straints, automated model generation, and optimization methods
for identifying attack paths that require fewer actions or expose
more significant security risks.
6.3
Architectural Countermeasures
The MASAR analysis provides a basis for identifying security
controls associated with the conditions represented in each attack
scenario. Rather than relying only on broad threat categories, the
proposed countermeasures focus on the signaling procedures and
trust boundaries relevant to the modeled weaknesses.
6.3.1
Mitigating Early Media Exploitation
The Early Media Free Ride scenario highlights the need to coor-
dinate SIP signaling states with media authorization and charging
policies.
Three security measures are relevant to this scenario:
•
Media Duration Controls: Network operators should
enforce configurable limits on early media sessions. When
the permitted duration is exceeded, the network should
apply the appropriate charging policy or terminate unau-
thorized media transmission.
•
State Dependent Media Authorization: Media gateways
and session border functions should enforce media autho-
rization policies according to session state and service
requirements. Early media transmission should remain
subject to the configured restrictions even when final SIP
session confirmation has not occurred.
•
Charging and Signaling Coordination: Charging mech-
anisms should account for media usage according to the
applicable service policy. This includes ensuring that pro-
longed provisional sessions do not bypass the charging
controls intended for the service.
These measures are consistent with the early media risks and
possible remedies described in RFC 3960 [28]. The SIP P-Early-
Media mechanism also provides a means to communicate early
media authorization policies within applicable trust domains [29].
The appropriate configuration depends on the operator’s ser-
vice model, charging architecture, and permitted early media
behavior. Consequently, restrictions should be selected according
to the deployment requirements rather than applying a universal
prohibition on bidirectional early media.
6.3.2
Securing the Diameter Cx Interface
The Unauthorized Diameter Cx Information Disclosure scenario
highlights the importance of controlling access to subscriber
authorization procedures and protecting sensitive information re-
turned through Diameter signaling.
The following measures address the relevant trust boundaries:
•
Diameter Peer Authentication and Authorization: Di-
ameter signaling infrastructure should accept authorization
requests only from authenticated and authorized peers. The
requesting entity’s identity and permitted signaling func-
tions should be validated before requests are forwarded
toward subscriber information services.
•
Signaling Boundary Enforcement: Diameter routing and
edge security functions should restrict access to internal

14
authorization services. Network segmentation, access con-
trol policies, and appropriate signaling protection mech-
anisms can reduce the ability of unauthorized entities to
submit subscriber queries.
•
Response Information Control: Authorization responses
should be handled according to the requesting peer’s
permissions and the applicable protocol requirements. Ac-
cess to subscriber status and internal routing information
should be restricted to entities authorized to receive it.
•
Monitoring of Authorization Requests: Security mon-
itoring can identify unusual patterns of subscriber au-
thorization queries, including repeated requests involv-
ing multiple subscriber identities or unexpected Diameter
peers.
These measures address the potential disclosure of subscriber
and routing information through insufficient signaling authoriza-
tion. Their implementation should follow the relevant Diameter
and IMS security requirements, including the procedures defined
in 3GPP TS 29.229 [31].
The proposed controls focus on the Diameter signaling trust
boundary rather than assuming that SIP subscriber authentication
must precede every legitimate Cx authorization procedure.

This process supports the classification of attack behavior and
the identification of candidate technique descriptions for telecom

6.5
Limitations and Future Research Directions
The MASAR evaluation focuses on two IMS signaling scenarios
and uses constraint-based models to examine selected security
properties. While these scenarios illustrate the proposed method-
ology, several limitations affect the interpretation and generaliz-
ability of the findings.
First, the current implementation follows an analyst-guided
process. STRIDE threat mapping, attack DAG construction, opera-
tional TTP mapping, and MiniZinc constraint model development
are performed manually. Although this approach supports trace-
able security analysis, it requires domain expertise and may in-
troduce analyst-dependent variations. Future work will investigate
automating these transformations and assessing their consistency
across different analysts and network configurations.
Second, the constraint models represent selected protocol
behaviors rather than complete IMS implementations. Their con-
clusions are limited by the defined states, transitions, assumptions,
and execution bounds. Validation in a representative IMS emu-
lation environment is therefore needed to determine whether the
modeled attack conditions correspond to observable behavior in
practical deployments.
Third, empirical scalability and performance measurements
are not yet available. Although the number of primary state
variables in the Early Media Billing Bypass model increases
linearly with the execution horizon, this does not imply linear
solver runtime or memory consumption. Future experiments will
evaluate the finalized MiniZinc models across different execution
bounds and configurations, recording solver runtime, memory
usage, and search statistics.
Fourth, operational TTP mapping requires further validation
against existing telecom threat knowledge bases and documented
attack behavior. Establishing consistent criteria for technique clas-
sification and novelty assessment remains an important direction
for future research.
To contextualize the formal verification approach, Table 8
summarizes the different analytical purposes and representations
of established formal methods and the constraint-based modeling
approach adopted by MASAR.
Unlike the general-purpose verification approaches summa-
rized in Table 8, MASAR uses MiniZinc constraint modeling to
represent selected protocol states and security properties derived
from architectural threat analysis and logical attack decomposition.
This supports bounded reasoning about potential unsafe states,
although comparative performance and verification effectiveness
have not yet been experimentally established.
Finally, extending the evaluation to additional telecom archi-
tectures, protocol implementations, and attack scenarios will help
assess the generalizability, reproducibility, and practical applica-
bility of MASAR.
