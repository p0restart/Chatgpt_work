P005 Abstract—To address cross-asset attack propagation and limited defense capacity in multi-asset networks, this paper establishes a complete-information, nonzero-sum, open-loop differential game. The network state is described by the compromised fraction of each asset to characterize how a defender dynamically deploys defensive resources. Continuous-time strategies are parameterized using piecewise-constant controls. By combining fourth-order Runge–Kutta integration, sequential least-squares programming, and alternating best responses, a dynamic defense action decision algorithm (ABR-DAD) is developed to calculate isolation and recovery intensities for each asset and time interval. A directed multi-asset propagation network is constructed to examine the spatiotemporal allocation of resources under different defense capacities. Dynamic defense, fixed-mean defense, and no defense are compared in terms of compromised states and cumulative losses under the same time-varying attack sequence. Simulations show that the computed plans satisfy per-time capacity constraints and yield differentiated resource allocation. Dynamic defense achieves lower cumulative weighted compromise loss and cumulative comprehensive defense loss than both reference schemes, demonstrating the effectiveness of the method for calculating dynamic defensive actions under finite resources.

P006 Keywords— network defense decision-making, nonzero-sum differential game, alternating best response, attack propagation, resource allocation

P007 Introduction

P008 A multi-asset network comprises interrelated logical assets, including entry services, business processing, identity authentication, and core data. Attacks can propagate from entry points to downstream assets along directed paths, causing compromise across multiple assets and business losses. A defender can suppress outgoing propagation through source-side isolation or reduce local compromise through recovery. These actions differ in security effects, business impacts, and resource consumption. Under limited capacity, the defender must account for the attacker’s strategy when determining the type, intensity, and timing of defensive actions.

P009 Regarding network propagation control and resource allocation, Preciado et al.[1] studied the allocation of protection resources to inhibit spreading processes in directed networks. Al-Eiadeh and Abdallah[2] allocated defense resources to interdependent systems using attack graphs and PageRank. Paarporn and Xu[3] examined resource tradeoffs between preventive and reactive defenses, while Chen et al.[4] investigated the effects of protection and recovery investments and node priorities on network robustness. These studies provide a basis for characterizing propagation structure, asset importance, and defense costs.

P010 In network attack–defense games, Shao and Li[5] used a multistage attack–defense graph to examine resource allocation during sequential attacks. Hu et al.[6] optimized moving-target defense through a Markov differential game, and Lou et al.[7] studied dynamic collaborative defense in crowdsensing networks under incomplete information. Huang and Zhu[8] used a susceptible–infected–susceptible (SIS) propagation model and a differential-game framework to study virus suppression and adaptive adjustment of connection weights in complex networks. Mi et al.[9] introduced stochastic disturbances into network attack–defense state evolution and developed a stochastic differential-game model for defense strategy selection. Tian et al.[10] studied a nonzero-sum attack–defense game for malware propagation in cloud environments. Zhang et al. investigated real-time defense in scale-free networks[11] and potential differential-game decisions in complex networks[12]. Sun et al.[13] incorporated action and timing choices into a time differential game. These works provide methodological foundations for analyzing strategic interactions alongside network-state evolution.

P011 For post-attack repair, Yang et al.[14] employed a differential game to study host repair under advanced persistent threats (APTs). Wang et al.[15] examined APT lateral movement, repair delays, and resource constraints in time-varying networks. Qin et al.[16] studied defense outsourcing under limited budgets through an impulsive differential game. Existing work thus covers propagation control, strategic interaction, and dynamic repair. This study further addresses the joint spatiotemporal allocation of source-side isolation and local recovery in a directed propagation network under attacker strategy responses and finite capacity constraints.

P012 This paper undertakes three tasks: constructing a nonzero-sum, open-loop differential-game model involving directed propagation, source-side isolation, and local recovery; designing ABR-DAD using piecewise-constant controls and alternating best responses to compute defensive actions by asset and interval; and analyzing allocations across defense capacities while comparing dynamic, fixed-mean, and no-defense plans under a common attack sequence in terms of compromised states and cumulative losses.

P013 Multi-Asset Network Attack–Defense Differential-Game Model

P014 Network State

P015 Represent the multi-asset network as a directed weighted graph G=V,E, where V={v1,…,vn} is the set of logical assets and E contains attack-propagation edges for which exploitation is feasible. Let βji≥0 be the effective propagation rate from source asset j to target asset i, with βji=0 when no edge exists and βii=0. Let xit∈0,1 denote the compromised fraction of the workload-instance group corresponding to asset i at time t. It is an aggregate measure of compromise among instances of the same workload type and describes the network state.

P020 Game Model

P021 A complete-information, two-player, noncooperative, nonzero-sum, open-loop differential game is defined on the continuous-time state system. At the planning origin, both players know the network topology, initial state, model parameters, objective functions, and constraints, and separately choose open-loop control plans over the horizon.

P022 Definition 1 (Multi-asset network attack–defense differential game). The game comprises players, a planning horizon, strategy sets, network state, state-transition function, and objective functions, written as the six-tuple Γ=N,T,P,x,f,J:

P023 1) Players: N={ND,NA}. The defender selects isolation and recovery, while the attacker selects entry attack intensities; each evaluates strategies according to its own objective.

P024 2) Horizon: T=0,T, where T>0 is the terminal time and horizon length. At t=0, the players specify open-loop plans over T. Controls may vary in time while the network state evolves continuously.

P025 3) Strategy sets: P=U,A, where U and A are the feasible defender and attacker open-loop strategy sets satisfying control bounds, entry restrictions, and capacity constraints. The defender’s plan comprises time-varying isolation and recovery controls.

P026 4) State: xt∈0,1n is the vector of compromised fractions for all assets, jointly determined by attack inputs, defensive actions, and propagation.

P027 5) State transition: xt=ft,xt,ut,at is specified by (1)–(2) and describes continuous compromise evolution and cross-asset dependencies.

P028 6) Objectives: J=JD,JA comprises the defender’s cumulative comprehensive loss and the attacker’s cumulative net payoff, with distinct state weights and action costs.

P035 Instantaneous capacity limits simultaneous inputs, while business impact and quadratic control costs accumulate over time. Consequently, the defender need not exhaust capacity in every interval. Under Definition 1, the players optimize entire-horizon plans over their known information and feasible strategies. Dynamic noncooperative game theory[17] supplies a criterion for their rational strategic relationship.

P038 Dynamic Defense Action Computation

P039 Piecewise-Constant Controls and Numerical Best Responses

P040 Using control parameterization[18], divide 0,T uniformly into K intervals with length Δt=T/K. For k=1,…,K, let ai,k, si,k, and ri,k remain constant over interval k, while xt evolves continuously according to (2). Denote the finite-dimensional decision variables by zD={si,k,ri,k} and zA={ai,k∣i∈IA}, with feasible sets UK and AK.

P042 Here v and b are candidate defender and attacker plans, and JD and JA are the cumulative loss and net payoff defined in Section II. Equations (13)–(14) describe each player’s best response to a fixed opponent plan and form the basis of alternating optimization. Fourth-order Runge–Kutta (RK4) integration[19] evaluates candidate plans; sequential least-squares programming (SLSQP) solves the single-player optimization with control bounds and linear capacity constraints[20,21]. The plans are then updated alternately. The final strategies undergo deviation searches from multiple initializations, constraint checks, and integration-refinement tests. The state equation evaluates compromise evolution under a control plan, while the finite-dimensional controls correspond directly to the defense locations, intervals, and intensities that the defender must determine.

P043 ABR-DAD Defense Action Computation

P044 Based on the best-response principle for dynamic noncooperative games[17] and related iterative work[22], we design Alternating Best Response for Dynamic Defense Action Decision (ABR-DAD). Its purpose is to produce a defense action plan: holding the attack plan fixed, optimize isolation and recovery; then, holding the defense plan fixed, compute the attack response; and alternate until the numerical stopping conditions are met. The procedure is shown in Algorithm 1.

Complete alternating runs start from uniform feasible, zero-control, and random feasible plans, while each individual player optimization uses the current plan as its sole starting point. The stopping metrics are normalized discovered unilateral improvement ηp, projected-gradient stationarity residual statp, and response distance distp, where ηp=gapp/1+Jp for p∈{D,A}. Once both SLSQP solvers exit normally, stopping is declared if the maxima of the three metrics across both players do not exceed 10-7, 10-4, and 10-3, respectively. P062 Evaluation of Defense Effectiveness

P063 Under the common attack input defined in Section IV-A, three complementary metrics assess the execution outcomes of dynamic, fixed-mean, and no-defense schemes.

P064 1) Cumulative weighted compromise loss: Lsec=0Ti=16qixit dt, where qi is the asset-value weight and xit is its compromised fraction. The integral reflects both compromise severity and duration.

P065 2) Cumulative comprehensive defense loss: JD=Lsec+Cba, where Cba is the time integral of the isolation-induced business-impact term in (3) and the quadratic action costs in (4). Since the terminal-state weight is zero in this experiment, the metric equals the defender’s objective in Section II and accounts for both security losses and defense costs.

P066 3) Terminal network value-weighted compromised fraction: XqT=i=16qixiTi=16qi. This metric captures the terminal network condition, distinct from the two cumulative-loss metrics. Together with key-asset trajectories, it characterizes asset protection.

P067 Figure 4 shows the API gateway, identity authentication, core database, and network value-weighted compromised fractions under the same attack sequence; Table 3 summarizes the metrics. At the beginning of interval 6 (t≈8.33), gateway entry attack intensity declines from 0.384142 to 0.139831 and Web entry intensity from 0.365943 to 0.137174. Under all three schemes, newly induced compromise pressure on the gateway falls below the corresponding recovery effect, changing its compromised fraction from increasing to decreasing. Identity authentication and the core database remain affected by upstream propagation and continue to accumulate compromise, illustrating propagation lag.

P068 Dynamic defense allocates more isolation and recovery to the gateway during early and middle intervals, reducing accumulated compromise earlier. In the final interval, its gateway recovery intensity is 0.080739, below approximately 0.262580 for fixed-mean defense. Consequently, the gateway compromised fraction decreases faster under the fixed scheme and becomes lower than under the dynamic scheme after t≈8.49. A late crossover also occurs for identity authentication, whereas the core database maintains a lower compromised fraction under dynamic defense. The network value-weighted compromised fraction is lower under dynamic defense during most of the horizon, but slightly lower under fixed defense at the terminal time. Cumulative loss and terminal state must therefore be distinguished.

P072 At the terminal time, the fixed scheme has a slightly lower network weighted compromised fraction than the dynamic scheme, and both improve on no defense. Overall, the computed dynamic plan reduces cumulative compromise and comprehensive defense losses by appropriately timing its actions, without implying minimum compromise for every asset at every instant.

P073 Conclusion

P074 This paper establishes a nonzero-sum, open-loop differential-game model that incorporates rational attack choices and cross-asset propagation, and proposes ABR-DAD for dynamic defense action decision-making. Simulations show that the algorithm produces differentiated allocations across time intervals and assets under all three defense capacities. Under the same attack sequence, dynamic defense reduces cumulative comprehensive defense loss relative to both no defense and fixed-mean defense, and mitigates compromise accumulation at entry and key internal assets during most intervals. Late crossovers in certain asset states further indicate the need to evaluate defense plans using a comprehensive objective encompassing time-accumulated compromise and action costs. Future work will calibrate propagation rates and defense costs using measured security-operation data and extend network scale and execution feedback to assess defense-plan updates under real operational conditions.
