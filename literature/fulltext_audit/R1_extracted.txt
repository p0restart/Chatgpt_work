 A B S T R A C T
In modern society, cloud computing has emerged as an indispensable infrastructure. Nevertheless, as the cloud 
ecosystem grows increasingly vast and complex, a series of novel security challenges have surfaced, among 
which artificial intelligence (AI)-empowered advanced malware has provided network attackers with even 
more stealthy and potent weapons. While existing malware detection technologies can still maintain a certain 
level of defense against traditional security threats, the instant detection and response to these sophisticated 
AI-crafted threats become exceedingly difficult, consuming substantial remediation time and security resources. 
To address the balance between control costs and effectiveness, recognizing the intricately intertwined and 
dynamically interactive nature of the offensive and defensive parties, this paper introduces the framework 
of differential game theory, delving into the strategies for controlling the propagation of advanced malware 
in cloud environments. Firstly, we construct an advanced malware propagation control model targeting each 
virtual machine. On this basis, we define the specific categories of strategy selection for both the offensive 
and defensive sides, as well as their respective cost-benefit relationships, and formulate an attack-defense 
game problem. Subsequently, we rigorously demonstrate, from a mathematical theoretical perspective, that 
the optimal solution (i.e., Nash equilibrium) to the attack-defense game problem is indeed attainable, and 
we devise a dedicated accelerated algorithm for its solution. Finally, we conduct comparative experiments on 
three real-world datasets using three distinct strategies, and the analysis results show the effectiveness of our 
proposed method.
1. Introduction
With the gradual advancement of digital transformation, cloud com-
puting and its applications have become information technology (IT) 
infrastructure. Indeed, this transformation has brought users unprece-
dented advantages such as scalability and flexibility, but the increasing 
complexity of cloud environments has also posed new security chal-
lenges [1]. For example, virtualization technology, as one of the core 
driving forces in cloud computing, has profound significance in com-
pletely overturning time and space limitations of traditional computing 
resources [2]. Through virtual machines (VMs), flexible and efficient 
solutions are tailored for diverse application scenarios, accurately con-
necting and meeting the diverse needs of users [3,4]. However, while 
enjoying unprecedented convenience and flexibility, there are also a 
series of potential security risks that cannot be ignored, which pose a 
serious challenge to the stability and secure operation of cloud environ-
ments [5]. According to a research report by Check Point, cloud-based

security situation, it is not only necessary to have a deep understanding

Undoubtedly, malware detection technology has demonstrated sig-
nificant effectiveness in the current security landscape, continuously 
attracting profound attention from academia [12]. Nevertheless, in the 
face of increasingly sophisticated and diversified advanced malware 
empowered by AI, particularly the emergence of novel threats such as 
zero-day attacks, existing detection methods struggle to contain their 
spread across networks [13], let alone accurately quantify the propaga-
tion patterns, control costs, and actual effects of advanced malware in 
cyberspace [14]. In response to this challenge, numerous scholars have 
innovatively drawn upon strategies for controlling biological infectious 
diseases, turning to the powerful tool of mathematical modeling to 
delve into the control mechanisms of malware propagation [15]. This 
interdisciplinary research perspective aims to uncover the dynamic 
behavioral patterns of malware within the network ecosystem by con-
structing precise mathematical models [16]. However, it is noteworthy 
that most current research tends to be confined to a single perspective 
either emphasizing the optimization of defensive strategies or focusing 
on analyzing the behavioral patterns of attackers, rarely placing both 
within the same framework to comprehensively consider their mutual 
interactions and influences. This limitation in perspective may hinder 
the comprehensiveness and practicality of research outcomes, as in 
real-world cyber offensive-defensive confrontations, attack and defense 
are intricately interconnected and dynamically shaped by each other.
Concerning the above discussions, this paper focuses on employing 
mathematical modeling techniques to deeply explore effective control 
strategies for the propagation of advanced malware in cloud envi-
ronments. Specifically, given the complex and dynamic interactions 
between attackers and defenders, especially when attackers leverage 
AI technologies to enhance their capabilities, we adopt an attack-
defense game framework to describe the evolution of attack-defense 
confrontations. By constructing a detailed game model, we not only 
consider the diversity of strategic choices for both parties but also delve 
into the profound impact of AI-empowered attack strategies on the 
formulation of defense strategies. Subsequently, mathematical proofs 
and solution algorithms are applied to conduct a meticulous analysis 
of this attack-defense game, aiming to theoretically reveal an optimal 
combination of attack and defense strategies.
The essential contributions of this paper are succinctly outlined 
below.
(1) We propose an advanced malware propagation control model 
for each virtual machine (VM) that can accurately portray the 
dynamic interplay between attacks launched by AI-empowered 
advanced malware and defense mechanisms within a cloud en-
vironment.
(2) We meticulously articulate the precise scopes of strategic choices 
undertaken by both the attacking and defending parties, along 
with their respective cost-benefit relationships, thereby formu-
lating a comprehensive attack-defense game problem frame-
work.
(3) We conclusively prove the achievability of the optimal solution 
in the attack-defense game problem and develop a tailored algo-
rithm that can efficiently accelerate the process of discovering 
this optimal solution.

the conclusions.
2. Related work
With the rapid advancements in digital technology, malware has 
quietly established itself as a significant challenge within cloud com-
puting. To effectively counter this threat, malware detection technology 
continues to ignite profound interest and in-depth research within the 
academic community. Rao and Jain [17] conducted a detailed malware 
analysis in cloud environments. Patil et al. [18] developed a hybrid 
architecture for securing VMs of the cloud. Nissim et al. [19] proposed a 
dedicated method specifically designed for trusted and secure detection 
of emerging and unidentified malware instances on VMs in private 
cloud infrastructure. Due to the rapid development and widespread 
application of AI technology, Patel et al. [20] suggested adopting 
machine learning methods to prevent cloud malware attacks. In con-
trast, Gao et al. [21] adopted the semi-supervised transfer learning 
method. Additionally, the work [22–26] also applied deep learning 
techniques for malware detection to improve the accuracy of malware 
identification.
However, the field of malware is experiencing an unprecedented 
wave of evolution over time [27], particularly the rise of advanced 
malware empowered by AI. This new type of threat greatly exacer-
bates the difficulty of defense, forcing organizations to invest huge 
repair time and security budgets to respond [9]. With its intricately 
designed complexity and innovation, advanced malware employs diver-
sified infiltration strategies and technical means to penetrate systems 
silently [6]. They cleverly evade detection by traditional security de-
fenses. They can even penetrate sandbox environments specifically 
constructed for dynamic malware analysis, demonstrating remarkable 
stealth and adaptability that pose unprecedented challenges to network 
security protection [11].
In light of this, to address the shortcomings of malware detection 
technology, scholars have studied the propagation behavior of mal-
ware in cloud environments. Against this backdrop, Abazari et al. [2] 
demonstrated remarkable ingenuity by pioneering the introduction of 
the concept of epidemic modeling from biological epidemiology into 
the realm of cloud computing. They creatively proposed a malware 
dynamical model tailored for cloud environments, thereby opening 
up a new avenue for deepening our understanding of and effectively 
containing the proliferation of malware in the cloud. On this basis, 
Gan et al. [16] extended the malware dynamical model to an infected 
cloud environment. Later, Del Rey et al. [28] used the mathematical 
modeling method to study the spread of advanced malware over ran-
dom complex networks, Dong et al. [29] developed a SIRS malware 
propagation model tailored for a controlled fractional network, offering 
an improved understanding of how malicious entities attack wireless 
sensor networks. However, these studies do not consider the cost and 
effectiveness of controlling the spread of malware. To this end, Tian 
et al. [14] proposed an optimal control model for malware propagation 
in the cloud, but only from a defensive perspective, ignoring the 
influence of the attacking side. Also, the work [30–32] investigated the 
optimal control of malware propagation but not in cloud environments. 
Considering the mutual influence between the offensive and defensive 
sides, the work [33–37] attempted to simulate malware propagation 
control using game theory. Unfortunately, they do not take into account 
cloud environments. Additionally, Gill et al. [38] examined current 
research and investigated how game theory models can be applied 
to tackle security challenges in cloud environments. Gill et al. [39] 
presented a non-cooperative game theory model designed to aid In-
trusion Detection Systems (IDS) in a cloud environment, analyzing the 
strategies employed by both attackers and defenders. Consequently, 
game theory models offer potential solutions for malware control in 
cloud environments.

3. Advanced malware propagation control model
This section will provide a detailed introduction to the advanced 
malware propagation control model, including background and moti-
vation, parameter symbol definition, and model description.
3.1. Background and motivation
With the swift advancements in information technology, cloud 
computing has played a pivotal role in the daily lives of individ-
uals by leveraging virtual technology to provide users with an all-
encompassing, flexible, and convenient service platform. However, 
concurrently, attacks targeting cloud environments have become in-
creasingly rampant, posing a security challenge that cannot be ignored. 
Although existing malware detection technologies can effectively resist 
traditional threats to some extent, they are inadequate when facing 
advanced malware empowered by AI. Such advanced malware is not 
only difficult to detect promptly but also capable of stealthily prop-
agating within the cloud, with its complex behavioral patterns and 
dynamics making it a significant challenge to balance control costs and 
effectiveness.
In light of this, this paper takes an innovative approach by introduc-
ing the methodology of epidemic spread modeling, aiming to deeply 
analyze and effectively control the spread of cloud-advanced malware 
through this interdisciplinary perspective. Furthermore, it ingeniously 
integrates game theory principles, considering the strategic interactions 
between security defenders and attackers, to construct a more realistic 
and dynamically evolving prevention and control model. This endeavor 
not only helps uncover the underlying propagation mechanisms of 
advanced malware in cloud environments but also more precisely 
delineates the delicate relationship between cost control and protection 
effectiveness, offering novel insights and solutions for research and 
practice in cloud security.

3.3. Model description
Cloud services rely extensively on virtualization technology as their 
solid foundation, with the core of this technology centered on the 
construction and flexible deployment of VMs. However, while this fea-
ture provides users with the convenience of installing diverse software 
applications and uploading a plethora of content such as documents, 
images, and videos, it also inadvertently creates potential avenues 
for the concealment and dissemination of malware within the cloud 
environment.
Similar to the research scopes of Refs. [2,16], we also focus on 
the propagation mechanisms of malware within VM environments. 
However, our research approach adopts a more refined path. Specif-
ically, rather than analyzing VMs as a generalized category, we delve 
into each VM as an independent unit of study. This strategy enables 
us to capture and characterize, with unprecedented granularity, the 
specific state changes of each VM at different time points, thereby

4. Attack-defense game problem
Given that both attackers and defenders pursue the minimization 
of costs and maximization of benefits, this dynamic process precisely 
aligns with the core principles of game theory. To cope with this 
offensive and defensive process, we need to understand various attack 
and defense strategies, as well as the consequences they may bring. 
Therefore, based on the established advanced malware propagation 
control model, we will systematically and thoroughly analyze this 
offensive and defensive process.
4.1. Definition of attack and defense strategies
Attack strategy encapsulates the multifaceted actions employed by 
an attacker to breach the defenses of a system. It spans a spectrum, 
commencing with rudimentary probing maneuvers and escalating to 
intricate malware assaults. Conversely, defense strategy outlines the 
proactive objectives pursued by the defender, centered on identifying 
and mitigating potential threats before they can inflict harm. This 
necessitates the deployment of a robust security arsenal, encompassing 
firewalls to erect barriers, antivirus software to neutralize malicious 
content, and monitoring systems to maintain vigilance. The ultimate 
aim of the defender’s strategy is to swiftly detect and thwart attacks, 
swiftly recover from any breaches that occur, and minimize the overall 
impact and potential losses incurred.

4.2. Definition of attack and defense payoffs
Now, let us further refine the quantitative analysis of attack and 
defense payoffs. Attack payoffs, serving as the quantitative indicator of 
attack effectiveness, measure the degree and efficiency of attackers in 
achieving their predetermined goals. Specifically, attack payoffs refer 
to the total revenue generated by attack actions, net of the costs re-
quired to implement such actions. In contrast, defense payoffs focus on 
evaluating the effectiveness of the defender in the face of potential or 
actual attacks. Here, defense payoffs do not directly refer to the positive 
income generated by defensive actions but rather to the total amount of 
losses reduced or avoided through effective defense strategies. In other 
words, defense payoffs represent the potential losses that the defender 
avoids due to successfully withstanding attacks. This value, combined 
with the costs invested in implementing defense measures, constitutes 
the overall economic consideration of defensive actions.

5.2. Nash equilibrium solving algorithm
Although Nash equilibrium can be solved from the above necessity 
system under certain conditions, the actual solving process may be 
time-consuming. To quickly find the Nash equilibrium, we specifically 
design a Nash equilibrium solving algorithm (see Algorithm 1) using 
Forward–Backward Euler methods [43].
Algorithm 1 fundamentally embodies an iterative framework.
Within each iteration cycle, we initially leverage the system dynamics 
described in (4) to prospectively simulate the evolution of equipment 
states. Subsequently, we employ the system (21) in a backward pass 
to derive updated adjoint functions. Finally, we integrate the systems 
(23), (25), (27), and (29) to compute a novel strategy combination. 
The iterative process concludes when either two consecutive strategy 
combinations converge significantly or the iteration count attains a 
predetermined threshold, signifying sufficient exploration.
The proposed Nash equilibrium solving Algorithm 1 is essentially an 
iterative process. In the algorithm’s execution, the initial step involves 
discretizing the period [0, 𝑇 ] with a uniform interval ℎ. The time 
complexity of this discretization step is 𝑂(𝑇 ∕ℎ). During each iteration, 
the algorithm requires traversing the network adjacency matrix. Since 
the adjacency matrix is a two-dimensional matrix of size 𝑁 × 𝑁, where 
𝑁 denotes the number of network nodes, the time complexity for this 
traversal is 𝑁 2. The iteration process continues until convergence or the 
predefined maximum number of iterations 𝐾 is reached. Consequently, 
the time complexity of this algorithm is 𝑂(𝐾𝑇 𝑁 2∕ℎ).

6.2. Comparison methods
To further underscore the effectiveness of our approach, we pit 
it against a random strategy, a uniform strategy, and an uncertainty 
attack strategy. Now, let us introduce these three different strategies.
(1) Random strategy: It refers to a system where the allocation 
of cost resources for both attack and defense is determined 
randomly at each control time step. This implies that both 
the attacker and the defender have the liberty to choose their 
strategies in an arbitrary, non-deterministic manner.
(2) Uniform strategy: It embodies a systematic approach that en-
sures the equitable and balanced distribution of constant control 
resources among all nodes within each time unit. This implies 
that both the attacker and the defender must maintain a long-
term stable and consistent strategic posture, without making any 
adjustments or changes to their strategies during this period.
(3) Uncertainty attack strategy: It refers to situations where, due 
to a lack of information and limited intelligence, malware attack-
ers may be unable to accurately estimate their gain functions. In 
such circumstances, malware attackers may be unable to identify 
the Nash equilibrium strategy combination and may instead opt 
for random or uniform attack strategies.
To effectively implement these strategies, we meticulously design 
corresponding algorithms, with detailed specifications outlined in Al-
gorithms 2 and 3, which are designed for generating random strategy 
pairs and unified strategy pairs. It is straightforward to observe that

When confronted with uncertain strategies, adopting random or uni-
fied strategies as coping strategies can be achieved by simply calling 
Algorithms 2 and 3, so there is no need to design additional specialized 
algorithms to handle such situations.

7. Conclusions
In this paper, we have constructed an attack defense game model 
aimed at exploring the complex dynamics of advanced malware prop-
agation control in cloud computing environments. We not only rigor-
ously demonstrated theoretically how both the offensive and defensive 
sides can achieve the possibility of Nash equilibrium while pursu-
ing the goals of minimizing costs and maximizing benefits, but also 
innovatively designed an efficient algorithm to achieve a fast and 
accurate solution to the Nash equilibrium. To show the effectiveness 
of our approach, we carefully planned a series of experiments, selected 
three representative real-world datasets, and adopted three differentia-
tion strategies as comparison benchmarks. The experimental outcomes 
conclusively demonstrate that our proposed method surpasses other 
strategies, not only effectively curbing the spread of advanced malware, 
but also achieving an ideal balance between cost control and protection 
effectiveness.
Although a detailed overview of our method has been provided 
earlier, there is still much work to be explored in future research. On 
the one hand, we need to continuously improve the practicality of the 
model to ensure that it can effectively guide practical applications and 
achieve a close integration of theory and practice. On the other hand, 
considering that the real transmission data of malware is not limited to 
network structure information, it is particularly important to explore 
and obtain real transmission data of malware, which has immeasurable 
value for deepening our research.
